Korean Skincare for Sensitive Skin: How to Choose

How to read a Korean ingredient list for sensitive skin, what 향료 and 무향 mean, fragrance-free vs. unscented, and how to patch test before your face.

By The Seoulful Edit Editors / Published 2026-11-23
The short answer

Choose Korean skincare for sensitive skin by reading the ingredient list, not the front of the box. Look for 향료, the Korean word for fragrance, pick fragrance-free over unscented, and add one product at a time. Patch test each one on your inner arm, twice a day for 7 to 10 days, before it goes on your face.

If your skin stings, flushes or itches easily, the front of a Korean box will not tell you much. Words like “calming” or “for sensitive skin” are the brand’s description. The back label is where the facts are.

Korean labels follow a clear rule for fragrance. Learn three or four words and you can check a product in a minute.

Front-label claims are not a standard

The FDA says there is no federal standard or definition for “hypoallergenic,” “fragrance-free” or “for sensitive skin” in the US. It tells shoppers to read the ingredient list instead.

Treat Korean claims the same way. 저자극 (low-irritation) and 민감 피부 (sensitive skin) on a box are the brand’s words. The ingredient list shows what is actually inside.

How fragrance shows up on a Korean label

  • The full list is called 전성분 (jeon-seong-bun). Korean products print every ingredient on the box.
  • Fragrance is often one word: 향료 (hyang-nyo). A brand can list its fragrance blend under that single word.
  • Allergens inside the fragrance must be named. If the blend contains any of 25 fragrance allergens set by the MFDS, the label cannot say only 향료. It must also name each allergen above 0.001% in a leave-on product or 0.01% in a rinse-off product (rule as of Nov. 17, 2026. Sources: MFDS guideline and Korea Cosmetic Association FAQ. Rules can change, so check the current version.)
  • Essential oils count. The MFDS guideline says plant oils or extracts used for scent must have their allergens labeled too, Cosmorning reported. A “natural” scent is still fragrance.
  • Small sizes can skip it. Minis of about 50 mL or less may leave the allergen names off the box for lack of space, but the brand must post the information online, per the same guideline.

So if you avoid fragrance, scan the list for 향료 and for names like 리날룰 (linalool) or 리모넨 (limonene).

Fragrance-free vs. unscented

These are not the same, the American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) explains.

  • Fragrance-free: no fragrance, not even one you cannot smell.
  • Unscented: a fragrance is masked so you do not smell it. The AAD says the masking chemicals can irritate dry, sensitive skin.

In Korean stores you may see 무향 (mu-hyang), “no scent.” It tells you how the product smells. It does not promise there is no 향료 on the list, so check.

Patch test before your face

The AAD’s method takes 7 to 10 days. Plan for it, especially if you shop on a trip.

  • Pick a quarter-sized spot where it will not rub off, such as the inside of your arm or the bend of your elbow.
  • Apply it twice a day at your normal amount.
  • Leave it on as long as you normally would. For a cleanser, about five minutes.
  • No redness, itching or swelling after 7 to 10 days? You can use it.
  • Know the normal tingle. The AAD notes that ingredients like retinol and glycolic acid can irritate sensitive skin, and that this is often temporary.

Keep the routine short

  • One new product at a time. The AAD warns that too many products, especially several anti-aging ones, can irritate skin.
  • Go easy on exfoliating. The AAD says sensitive, dry or acne-prone skin may do best with a washcloth and a mild chemical exfoliant, not a scrub. With rosacea, it says to stop exfoliating.
  • Check your toner. A Cleveland Clinic dermatology resident advises people with sensitive skin to avoid toners that are heavily fragranced or use a lot of dyes and preservatives. Our Korean toner guide explains how toners and toner pads differ.
  • Moisturize. The AAD recommends gentle, fragrance-free products for dry, sensitive skin. See our Aestura vs. Illiyoon moisturizer guide for two Korean options, and what cica is if you keep seeing it on calming-feeling products.

A simple order to follow

Cleanser, toner if you like it, moisturizer, then sunscreen in the morning. Our Korean skincare steps guide explains why the order runs thin to thick.

Change one thing at a time. If your skin reacts, you will know which product to drop.

Korean words to look for on the back of the box

  • 전성분 (jeon-seong-bun): full ingredient list. Start here.
  • 향료 (hyang-nyo): fragrance. If you avoid fragrance, this is the word to find.
  • 리날룰 (linalool), 리모넨 (limonene), 벤질알코올 (benzyl alcohol), 신남알 (cinnamal): four of the 25 fragrance allergens Korean labels must name.
  • 무향 (mu-hyang): no scent. It describes the smell. Check the list for 향료 anyway.
  • 저자극 (jeo-ja-geuk): low-irritation, a claim the brand makes.
  • 민감 피부 (min-gam pi-bu): sensitive skin.

Screenshot this list. In a store, show it to a staff member and ask to see the back label.

Shopping in Seoul with sensitive skin

  • Photograph the back label before you buy. A translation app can read the 전성분 list for you.
  • Buy one new product, not five. You need time to patch test each one, and a trip is short.
  • Skip testers on your face. Try a store tester on your inner arm, not your cheek.
  • Medicated creams are medicines. Korean pharmacies sell ointments and creams for skin problems. Ask the pharmacist, or your dermatologist at home, before you use one. See our Korean pharmacy guide.

If your skin reacts

  • Wash it off gently as soon as you can, and do not use it again, the AAD says.
  • Soothe it. The AAD suggests a cool compress or petroleum jelly if needed.
  • See a dermatologist if the reaction is severe or does not settle.
  • Report it in the US. The FDA asks people who think they had a reaction to a cosmetic to report it to the agency and talk with their healthcare provider.

Frequently Asked Questions

What Korean skincare is good for sensitive skin?

There is no single brand that suits all sensitive skin. Look for fragrance-free products with a short ingredient list, and add one new product at a time. The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) recommends fragrance-free products for dry, sensitive skin and suggests testing each new product on your arm for 7 to 10 days.

What is the difference between fragrance-free and unscented?

Fragrance-free means the product has no fragrance at all. Unscented means a fragrance has been masked so you cannot smell it, the AAD explains, and the masking chemicals can still irritate dry, sensitive skin. The FDA adds that there is no federal standard for the term fragrance-free, so check the ingredient list too.

How do I patch test new skincare?

Apply the product to a quarter-sized spot on the inside of your arm or the bend of your elbow, twice a day for 7 to 10 days, the AAD says. Use your normal amount. If you see no redness, itching or swelling, you can use it. If you react, wash it off and do not use it again.
