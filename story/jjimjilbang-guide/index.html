Korean Jjimjilbang Guide: How a Visit Works, Step by Step

Your first Korean jjimjilbang, step by step: shoe lockers, the uniform, the gender-separated baths, sauna rooms, sikhye and eggs, sleeping over, and etiquette.

By The Seoulful Edit Editors / Published 2026-11-26
The short answer

A Korean jjimjilbang is a bathhouse with gender-separated baths and shared heated sauna rooms. You store your shoes, take a locker key, shower and bathe naked in the baths, then put on the loose uniform for the common sauna rooms, snack bar and sleeping area. Many stay open 24 hours.

A jjimjilbang (찜질방) is a Korean bathhouse with heated rooms. You bathe, sweat in sauna rooms, eat, nap and sometimes stay the night, all in one building.

The Korea Herald describes it as a Korean-style sauna built on two old heating ideas: ondol, the heated floor, and gama, dome-shaped kiln rooms. The Korea Times notes that many are open 24 hours. It feels strange the first time. After that, it is simple.

How a visit works, step by step

  1. Take off your shoes. Do it at the entrance and put them in a small shoe locker. Keep the key.
  2. Pay at the counter. You hand over the shoe key, or show it, and get a locker key, a uniform and towels. The Korea Herald describes this order at one jjimjilbang.
  3. Go to your side. Men and women split here into separate locker rooms and baths.
  4. Undress fully. The baths are naked-only, Korea.net notes. Everyone around you is doing the same.
  5. Shower before you soak. Wash your whole body and hair at the shower stations. This is the rule that matters most.
  6. Bathe. Move between hot, warm and cold pools at your own pace. Drink water.
  7. Put on the uniform. It is usually a loose T-shirt and shorts. Now you can go to the shared floor.
  8. Use the sauna rooms. Men and women mix here, all in uniform.
  9. Eat, rest, sleep. Then shower again, return the uniform and towels, and pay any extras when you hand back your key.

The locker key does a lot

  • Wear it. VisitKorea notes that the key usually goes on your wrist or ankle.
  • It is your wallet. At many places it also pays for snacks and massage chairs, and you settle the total at the counter when you leave.

The sauna rooms

  • Different themes. Rooms are lined with clay, salt, stone, charcoal or jade, and some are cold rooms for cooling down.
  • Start cool. VisitKorea suggests first-timers start in rooms around 30 to 40°C.
  • Short stays in the hottest rooms. For rooms above 60°C, VisitKorea advises keeping it to about 20 minutes. Some kiln rooms ask you to cover your skin with a towel and wear clogs, the Korea Herald reports.
  • Leave if you feel dizzy. Rest, drink water and cool down.

Food: sikhye and eggs

  • Sikhye (식혜). A cold, sweet rice drink. The Korea Herald calls it a traditional drink made with rice and malt.
  • Roasted eggs. Eggs slow-cooked in the heat, called kiln-cooked eggs in the Korea Herald. VisitKorea calls sikhye with roasted eggs a popular, affordable pair.
  • Real meals too. Snack bars and restaurants sell noodles, soups and more. Some places let you bring food, others don’t, so check.

The towel hat

You will see people with a hand towel rolled into two side buns, called yangmeori (양머리), or “sheep head.” VisitKorea notes it is a familiar image from Korean media. It keeps your hair up and away from the heat. Roll a towel in thirds lengthwise, then roll each end in toward the middle, and set it on your head.

Sleeping over

The Korea Times describes napping or staying overnight on the heated floor among strangers as an ordinary Korean scene. Some places have mats or a quiet sleeping room. Keep valuables in your locker and ask at the counter whether staying overnight costs extra.

Tattoos

Rules vary by place. The Korea JoongAng Daily reports that some hotel and golf-course saunas refuse visible tattoos or ask guests to cover them with patches. If you have tattoos, check the counter sign or ask before you pay.

Entry prices and hours change, so check them at the counter or on the place’s Naver Map page.

Etiquette in short

  • Shower first, every time. Wash fully before any pool.
  • Keep towels out of the water. Tie long hair up.
  • No phones or photos in the bath and locker areas.
  • Keep your voice low in sauna rooms and sleeping areas.
  • Don't stare. Nudity is ordinary here.
  • Return what you borrow. Uniforms and towels go in the marked bins.

Adding a seshin body scrub

Many bathhouses offer a paid body scrub, called seshin (세신) or ttaemiri (때밀이). It is optional. CNN Travel's interviews with Seoul scrub attendants describe it like this:

  • Same-gender attendants scrub you in the bath area.
  • Soak first, about 30 minutes.
  • Bar soap before, not body wash.
  • About 40 minutes on a table, then a rinse.

Ask at the counter or the bath area. For doing it yourself with a scrub mitt, see our Italy towel guide.

What to bring

  • Not much. Towels and the uniform come with entry.
  • Your own toiletries if you are picky. Many places have basic soap, and some sell small packs.
  • A hair tie.
  • A card or cash for extras. Some places charge snacks to your locker key.
  • Moisturizer for after the heat. See our Korean body care picks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do you have to be naked in a jjimjilbang?

Only in the baths. The bath and shower areas are separated by gender, and everyone bathes naked there. In the shared sauna rooms, lounges and sleeping areas, men and women mix, and everyone wears the loose T-shirt and shorts the jjimjilbang gives you.

Can you go to a jjimjilbang with tattoos?

It depends on the place. There is no national ban, and each business sets its own rule. The Korea JoongAng Daily reports that some hotel and golf-course saunas turn away tattooed guests or ask them to cover tattoos with patches. Check the sign at the counter, or ask before you pay.

Can you sleep overnight at a jjimjilbang?

Yes, at many of them. The Korea Times describes visitors napping or staying overnight on the heated floor among strangers. Some places have mats, blankets or a separate sleeping room. Not every jjimjilbang allows it, and rules on overnight stays differ, so ask at the counter.
