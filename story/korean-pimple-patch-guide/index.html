Korean Pimple Patch Guide: How to Use Acne Patches

What a Korean pimple patch can and can't do, hydrocolloid vs. microneedle patches, how to put one on, how labels differ in Korea and the US, and Daiso picks.

By The Seoulful Edit Editors / Published 2026-11-24
The short answer

A Korean pimple patch is a small sticker you put on a spot. Plain hydrocolloid patches cover a pimple that has a head or has opened, soak up fluid and keep your fingers off it. They don't reach deep, painful bumps. If breakouts keep coming back, see a board-certified dermatologist.

Pimple patches are one of the easiest Korean skincare buys to bring home. They are small, cheap and quick to use. In Seoul you’ll find them at Daiso, Olive Young and many pharmacies.

They are also easy to expect too much from. A patch covers a spot. It does not clear acne. Here is what a patch can do, which kind to pick and how to read the box.

What a pimple patch does

Most basic patches are made of hydrocolloid. The Korea Pharmaceutical Information Center describes hydrocolloid as a mix of gelatin, carboxymethylcellulose and pectin that turns into a gel as it takes up fluid.

On a pimple, that means three things:

  • It soaks up fluid from a spot that has a head or has opened. That is why the patch turns white.
  • It keeps your fingers off. The AAD says picking and popping make acne take longer to clear and raise your risk of scars and dark spots.
  • It hides the spot a little. Thin, clear patches sit flat under a mask or a hat.

What it doesn’t do

  • It won’t reach deep bumps. An AAD press release quotes dermatologist Fatima Fahs, MD: pimple patches “help draw out fluid from pimples and stop you from picking, but they won’t work on deep acne.”
  • It won’t stop new spots. For acne that keeps coming back, the AAD says to partner with a board-certified dermatologist.
  • It isn’t for an infected spot. The Korea Pharmaceutical Information Center says hydrocolloid dressings should not be used on infected wounds. If a spot is hot, swollen or getting worse, see a doctor.

Who should buy what

  • You pick at spots: plain hydrocolloid patches in a mixed-size pack.
  • You want to wear one in the day: a thin, clear patch. Look for 베벨링 (beveling) on the box, which means a tapered edge.
  • You want something fun for a weekend: printed patches, like the heart and star shapes at Daiso.
  • You have a small bump with no head: some people try a microneedle-style patch. Read the box for what the brand says it does, and remember a patch won’t reach deep bumps.

How to put one on

  1. Wash your hands and the spot. Pat it fully dry. Patches don’t stick to wet or oily skin.
  2. Pick a patch bigger than the spot. The edge should sit on clear skin.
  3. Press it on before skincare. Put the patch on clean skin, then apply your other products around it.
  4. Leave it on for the time on the box. Some boxes say overnight is fine.
  5. Peel it off slowly from the edge. Change it when it turns white and puffy.

If your skin turns red or itchy under the patch, stop using it.

At a pharmacy

Korean pharmacies sell patches next to acne creams and ointments. Some of those creams are medicines. Ask the pharmacist before you buy them, as we explain in our Korean pharmacy guide.

Fit patches into your routine

A patch is one small step. Keep the rest simple: a gentle cleanser, a moisturizer and sunscreen in the morning. The AAD says to give any acne treatment time to work, since it may take several weeks to a few months to see a difference. Hydrocolloid or microneedle: which patch for which spot

  • A whitehead or a spot that has opened: a plain hydrocolloid patch. It covers the spot and soaks up fluid.
  • A small bump with no head yet: many Korean brands sell microneedle or "micro" patches for this, often with skincare ingredients. Read the box for what the brand says it does.
  • A deep, sore bump under the skin: a patch won't reach it. The AAD notes that pimple patches won't work on deep acne.
  • Lots of spots at once, or spots that leave marks: see a dermatologist rather than buying more patches.

How to read the box in Korea

Look for one of three words. 의료기기 (medical device), 의약외품 (quasi-drug) or 화장품 (cosmetic). The word tells you how the product is regulated in Korea.

Why it matters. The Korea Pharmaceutical Information Center says Korea's drug regulator manages wound dressings (창상피복재) as medical devices, while bandages and plasters are quasi-drugs. A Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety answer says a patch meant to protect and care for skin with acne does not fit the definition of a cosmetic.

Where you'll see them. Daiso Mall lists some patches under household medical supplies (가정의료용품) and others under skincare spot care (스팟케어).

Before you pack them for home

  • US rules are different. The FDA says whether a product is a cosmetic or a drug depends on its intended use. A product made to treat acne is regulated as a drug.
  • Salicylic acid patches. The FDA's OTC acne monograph (M006, final order posted Nov. 23, 2021) lists salicylic acid at 0.5 to 2 percent as an acne active ingredient. US versions carry a Drug Facts label.
  • Plain patches pack flat. A sheet of patches weighs almost nothing and fits in a passport cover.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do pimple patches actually work?

They help with one job: covering a spot. An AAD press release quotes dermatologist Fatima Fahs, MD, saying pimple patches help draw out fluid and stop you from picking, but they won't work on deep acne. For acne that keeps coming back, the AAD suggests seeing a board-certified dermatologist.

How long should you leave a pimple patch on?

Follow the time on the box. Some people put a plain patch on at night and take it off in the morning. Change it when it turns white and puffy, since that means it has soaked up fluid. If your skin gets red or itchy under the patch, take it off and stop.

Are Korean pimple patches a medical device?

Some are and some aren't, so read the box. In Korea, a patch can be sold as a medical device, a quasi-drug or a cosmetic, depending on what it is made and claimed for. In the US, the FDA decides by intended use, and acne patches with salicylic acid fall under its OTC acne drug rules.
